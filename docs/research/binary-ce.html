Three Body Systems

Two stars inside on another star?!?

Three body systems are famously known from the "three body problem". Two stars are orbiting each other in a beautiful and understandable way. All of a sudden, a third body comes into the arrangement. The system now becomes chaotic and hard to understand. In fact, it is impossible to predict the motion analytically, with just mathematical equations. But three body systems don't always have to be chaotic. In this work, I look at hierarchical triple systems. These are systems where two of the bodies are orbiting really close to each other while another one orbits the two of them really far away. I like to think of these as the Sun, the Earth and the Moon. The Moon and the Earth are orbiting each other and together they orbit the third body, the Sun, that is very far away.

In the case here, instead of the Sun, the Earth and the Moon we have a massive star (analogous to the Sun) and two small stars (analogous to the Earth and the Moon). The massive star starts aging and reaches the life stage when it becomes a red giant. It grows so big that it reaches the two little stars and envelopes them. This stage is called a common envelope, because the envelope of the red giant (ie the outer layers of the massive star) is shared with a companion (in this cases, the two little stars are the companions). Then what happens to the little stars?

We found that these little stars will slowly get pulled apart when inside of the giant star. They get to a point where gravity cannot keep them together anymore. At this point, the system becomes chaotic (just like the well-known three body problem). We are interested in the final outcome of this system, but small changes on the velocities or locations of all three stars give complately different outcomes. At this point we decided to do a probabilistic study to know how often each of the outcomes are. Stay tuned for the results of these probabilities coming soon!
